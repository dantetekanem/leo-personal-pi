# Social Posts From Content

Create social copy from material the user already has. The job is not to summarize the source or advertise that it exists. Find the part that earns attention, preserve its truth, and write a post that sounds like a knowledgeable person sharing something worth reading.

## Start with the source

Read the complete source when available. If the user provides several references, identify which one is primary and use the others only to clarify or support it.

Extract five things before drafting:

1. **Purpose:** What should the reader understand, feel, question, or do?
2. **Strongest tension:** What conflict, surprise, consequence, or unresolved decision makes the source interesting?
3. **Concrete detail:** Which true example, number, result, failure, or observation makes the post credible?
4. **Reader value:** What will someone gain by reading the source rather than stopping at the social post?
5. **Author voice:** How does the source actually sound when it is direct, specific, and unforced?

Do not invent a personal experience, result, motivation, or causal story. A hypothetical example in the source must remain hypothetical.

## Respect the user's format

Treat explicit constraints as binding: link position, length, paragraph count, tone, point of view, punctuation, hashtags, emoji, calls to action, and platform.

Do not silently replace those choices with generic platform advice. If the user wants the link first, put it first. If the user wants one short paragraph, return one short paragraph.

When no format is specified, default to:

- one ready-to-paste version;
- a concise opening followed by one compact body;
- no hashtags or emoji unless they add clear value;
- no explanation before the copy;
- platform-neutral language that can travel well.

Honor established writing preferences already provided by the user, including punctuation choices. Do not add em dashes when the user has asked to avoid them.

## Choose one angle

A short social post cannot carry the whole source. Select one honest angle that represents it well:

- a concrete tension the source resolves;
- a practical problem the reader recognizes;
- a result and the lesson behind it;
- a personal observation supported by the source;
- a question the source answers;
- a change in how the author now thinks or works.

Choose the angle with the strongest combination of truth, relevance, and curiosity. Do not stack several hooks or compress every section into a miniature article.

## Build a meaningful progression

Strong short posts often move through a small chain of thought rather than a collection of punch lines:

1. Start with a concrete change in conditions, scale, or capability.
2. Explain what is no longer the difficult or interesting part.
3. Name the work, decision, or responsibility that remains.
4. Tell the reader what the source reveals about that remaining work.
5. Mention any supporting artifact only after the problem and value are clear.

A conditional opening such as “When you can…” can place the reader inside the changed reality without repeating first person or pretending that a hypothetical event happened to the author. Use this as a reasoning shape, not a sentence template. The final post should still sound native to its source.

## Write for attention without clickbait

The opening should earn the next line through specificity, tension, recognition, or consequence. It should not announce that the author "wrote a post" unless that is genuinely the most interesting fact.

A strong opening:

- begins with substance rather than promotion;
- uses a concrete fact or observation when the source provides one;
- creates an honest question in the reader's mind;
- signals the subject quickly;
- promises only value the source actually delivers.

Avoid empty hooks, exaggerated stakes, withheld facts, fake controversy, and generic claims. Curiosity should come from the source, not from hiding what the post is about.

## Give a reason to read

A social post should provide enough context to establish trust while leaving meaningful depth in the source.

State or imply what the reader will find there: a practical method, a hard-earned lesson, a decision framework, a detailed example, a useful tool, an argument, or a clearer way to see the problem.

Do not merely say "read more," "check it out," or "here are my thoughts." Do not summarize so completely that opening the source adds nothing.

## Sound human

Write as one person speaking clearly to another.

Prefer:

- concrete nouns and direct verbs;
- natural sentence rhythm;
- one earned opinion;
- details that could only come from the source;
- contractions and ordinary language when they fit the author's voice;
- a little roughness when polishing it away would remove personality.

Avoid:

- corporate language and marketing filler;
- article-abstract prose;
- manufactured punch lines;
- slogan pairs and symmetrical sentence stacks;
- repeated first-person openings;
- repeated restatements of the same thesis;
- generic phrases that could promote any article;
- excessive headings, bullets, or tiny paragraphs used only to simulate drama.

Read the draft aloud mentally. If it sounds like a campaign, keynote slide, or template, rewrite it as speech.

## Keep first person under control

First person can establish ownership, but repetition makes the post feel self-promotional and mechanical.

Use it only where the author personally did, learned, built, changed, or believes something important. Combine or recast sentences when several paragraphs begin with the same pronoun. Never remove the personal angle entirely when it is the source of credibility.

## Mention projects and products naturally

Include a project, tool, company, paper, or product only when it is part of the source's actual value. Explain its role in the idea instead of turning the post into a launch pitch.

Names should support the reader's understanding, not replace it.

## Adapt when a platform is named

Use current platform constraints and audience behavior only when they materially affect the result. Keep the source's meaning and the user's voice above algorithm folklore.

If current research is requested:

1. Prefer official guidance, primary research, and transparent data-backed analyses.
2. Separate robust findings from creator folklore and disputed algorithm claims.
3. Use research to shape the draft, not to make the copy sound researched.
4. Keep citations outside the ready-to-paste post unless the user asks for them inside it.

## Respond to feedback correctly

Treat rejection as evidence about the writing, not as a request for synonyms.

Capture the exact failure the user identified, such as:

- too generic;
- too polished;
- too much first person;
- wrong link placement;
- too much summary;
- not enough reason to read;
- invented personal context;
- product pitch instead of useful content;
- edited more than the highlighted text.

Change the underlying angle, structure, or level of specificity. Do not lightly paraphrase a rejected draft and present it again.

When the user highlights one sentence, change only that sentence unless the correction creates a real contradiction elsewhere. When repeated failures reveal a genuine preference decision that the source cannot settle, ask one focused question instead of guessing again.

## Final quality check

Before returning the copy, verify:

- Every factual claim comes from the source or the user.
- The post follows the requested link position and shape.
- The opening contains real substance.
- One concrete detail anchors the writing when available.
- The reader has a clear reason to continue or open the source.
- The post preserves context without retelling everything.
- First person is natural rather than repetitive.
- No sentence exists only to sound impressive.
- Known punctuation and style preferences are respected.
- The result sounds natural when read aloud.
- Cutting another 10% would not make it clearer.

## Output

Return the ready-to-paste social post first and without a preamble.

Provide alternatives only when requested. If the user asked for research, place a compact evidence note and public source links after the clean post, clearly separated so they are not copied accidentally.
